## 📋 POST BODY (copy this verbatim)

> A process engineer on a brownfield revamp project spent 11 days extracting instrument tags from 340 P&ID sheets.
>
> Manually. In Excel. Cross-referencing every loop number against the instrument index, chasing line sizes across revision-controlled drawings, checking valve types against the datasheet register.
>
> The AI version of that same job took 23 minutes.
>
> This isn't a lab result. It's a real project. And it raises a question every engineering team needs to sit with: how much senior engineer time is currently being consumed by work that AI can do in the time it takes to make a coffee?
>
> **What P&ID extraction actually costs you:**
>
> P&IDs are the backbone of process plant documentation. They contain instrument tags, line designations, valve specifications, equipment identifiers, control loops, and process flows — all encoded in dense engineering notation across hundreds of sheets.
>
> Every time a project needs that data in usable form — for HAZOP studies, Management of Change (MoC) reviews, procurement schedules, or commissioning dossiers — someone has to manually extract it.
>
> On a 200-sheet package, that's typically 5–8 engineer-days of a senior process or instrumentation engineer. The people who are hardest to replace and most expensive to mis-allocate.
>
> The errors that come with manual extraction compound it. A missed tag in the instrument index creates a procurement gap. A transposed line number in the line list creates a HAZOP blind spot. A valve misclassified in the datasheet register causes a design query that bounces for two weeks before someone traces it back to the source.
>
> **What AI extraction delivers:**
>
> The system processes P&ID sheets — scanned, PDF, or native CAD — and returns:
>
> → **Instrument tag register** — all tags, types, loop numbers, P&ID sheet reference, and revision automatically populated
>
> → **Line list** — line numbers, fluid service, pipe class, insulation type, size — extracted from title blocks and line designations across every sheet
>
> → **Valve schedule** — type, tag, size, and specification cross-referenced per sheet, ready for the procurement team
>
> → **Equipment list** — rotating and static equipment extracted with tag, description, and drawing reference
>
> → **MoC impact map** — on revision packages, the system flags every instrument, line, and equipment item affected by the revision, surfacing scope that manual reviewers commonly miss
>
> The output lands in a structured spreadsheet that drops directly into your document control system. No re-keying. No formatting. No QA loop on human transcription errors.
>
> **The real project data:**
>
> — 340-sheet brownfield instrument extraction: 11 engineer-days → 23 minutes
> — 180-sheet line list population: 4 days → 14 minutes
> — MoC impact review on 60-sheet revision package: 2 days → 31 minutes with 100% tag coverage (manual review had missed 7 affected instruments)
> — HAZOP preparation data pack: 3 days of P&ID markup → 2.5 hours including structured node list
>
> The instrumentation lead on the brownfield project said the same thing almost every engineer says when they see the output for the first time:
>
> "I want to know why we've been doing it the other way."
>
> **Where the value stacks:**
>
> Single-project ROI is obvious. The compound effect is bigger: when extraction is automated, your senior engineers stop being the bottleneck on data availability, MoC reviews move faster, HAZOP preparation compresses, and procurement has accurate data earlier in the project — the phase when errors are cheapest to fix.
>
> The engineering firms getting ahead aren't the ones with the best engineers.
>
> They're the ones whose engineers spend their time on engineering.
